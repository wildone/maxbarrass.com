What Could You Do With More Tokens?

By Max Barrass · 6 min read

In this essay

One of my GitHub screenshots reads “1097 contributions on October 9th.”

That is one day's recorded contribution activity. It is not a token count, a cost calculation or a measure of customer value. It does, however, make a useful question feel rather less theoretical: what would I choose to do with more capacity?

I have four or five projects running at once, with multiple agents and subagents across two laptops and two PCs, and work running around the clock. My personal factory uses off-the-shelf tools you can use too. More AI capacity could give me room to attempt work that currently waits.

My evolving, open-source approach gives each project a coordinating agent, implementation subagents and GitHub issues for persistent tracking. Reviewed pull requests, automated tests and deployment pipelines connect the work to acceptance. I use Forgejo for private work and deployments, and GitHub for public work and pipelines.

Update: 21 October 2026

An earlier screenshot I shared showed 83.1 billion lifetime cumulative tokens. The newer screenshots I shared for this update show 147 billion. These are snapshots of a cumulative interface counter, rather than amounts used in a particular day.

The newer view also reports a 9 billion peak, without specifying its period here; a “Longest task” of 60h 50m; a longest streak of 39 days; and a current streak of 21 days.

Those counters do not establish uninterrupted autonomous work, delivered outcomes or cost. They give me context for the question in this essay: what useful work would I choose to pursue with the capacity available?

Give the extra capacity a job

I am building my factory to chase dreams that would otherwise stay on the backburner. If more capacity became available tomorrow, I would start by choosing which idea deserves that room.

Which unfinished change is closest to being useful? Which awkward maintenance task keeps being displaced by a visible feature? Which assumption would you most like someone to challenge before the next release?

I would start with named work and a reason for doing it. “Improve the application” is an invitation to wander. “Check that a failed import leaves existing records intact, and show the evidence” gives a contributor something bounded to finish.

For each candidate, write down the intended result, who can accept it and what must be demonstrated. Then choose an order. Extra capacity is an opportunity to make a deliberate bet on useful work.

Finish the nearly finished

There is something seductive about starting another implementation. It creates visible movement quickly. A change awaiting its final checks can look much less exciting.

I would give that nearly finished work a serious claim on additional capacity. An agent could reproduce the reported behaviour, exercise the acceptance criteria, investigate a failing check or prepare the evidence a reviewer needs. The result might be a smaller queue of changes ready for a decision.

That matters across parallel projects. Every unfinished item retains assumptions and context that somebody must carry. Bringing one through acceptance can free attention for the next.

An accepted change is an output I can inspect. Its outcome needs observing in use: did it solve the intended problem? I want both recorded as I learn what extra capacity is worth directing towards.

Buy a more demanding examination

Extra capacity could fund the questions that a happy-path demonstration leaves unanswered.

What happens when a dependency times out halfway through an operation? When the same request arrives twice? When a user has insufficient permission? When the data is valid enough to enter the system but awkward enough to break an assumption?

Choose cases because they threaten a requirement or a credible failure mode. A large collection of easy assertions can consume effort while leaving the important uncertainty intact.

I would also consider an independent review pass. Give the reviewer the requirement, relevant context and implementation evidence, then ask it to look for unsupported assumptions and failure paths. Its findings still need checking. The useful output is an evidenced concern or a reasoned acceptance decision, rather than a reassuring verdict by itself.

Rehearse the changes you cannot casually undo

A migration is a good candidate for additional investigation before execution.

An agent could help prepare representative fixtures, compare results before and after a transformation, examine compatibility assumptions and rehearse recovery in an authorised test environment. The acceptance record should explain what remained intact, what changed and what would trigger a stop.

That is a possible use of capacity, not a claim that I have completed a particular migration. Production execution still needs its own authority and controls. More available reasoning does not grant permission to alter live data.

The attraction is practical: spend some capacity learning while the consequences are still contained.

Fund the work that makes the next change easier

Documentation and maintenance deserve a place in this conversation.

An additional agent could check whether setup instructions work from a clean environment, reconcile a runbook with current behaviour or turn an important decision into a usable handoff. Acceptance should involve following the instructions and checking the result. More pages alone would tell me very little.

Maintenance could include investigating a recurring build failure, simplifying a troublesome test fixture or assessing a dependency update. Select a concrete problem and verify the improvement. An enthusiastic sweep through unrelated files would make the next review harder.

I use VibeGov as policies ChatGPT follows. More capacity could help me extract reusable patterns, components and tooling from project work into the shared foundation I am developing. The aim is consistent workflows and less repeated setup, with project findings improving what the next can reuse.

Leave room to discover something

I would reserve some capacity for prototypes too.

A bounded experiment can answer whether an unfamiliar approach is worth pursuing. Name the question, set a stopping point and decide what evidence would justify the next step. A prototype that rules out an unsuitable direction can be a useful result.

Keep its status clear. A successful demonstration does not establish operational readiness. Decide separately whether to harden it, revise it or put it away.

On my tiny budget, I want to learn which choices produce useful outputs and outcomes. More tokens alone cannot predict those results; review, integration and decisions still matter. I would adjust the work as I learn.

What would you finish, test, repair or discover with more capacity? Try an experiment with the shared method, then compare what it produced and what you learnt. I wonder what others are doing with their resources and reusable foundations. Share your priorities, methods and observations. Documenting how we work can help each of us avoid repeating the same project setup.

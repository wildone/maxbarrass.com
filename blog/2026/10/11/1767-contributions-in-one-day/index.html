1,767 Contributions in One Day. What Does Your Organisation Deliver?

By Max Barrass · 5 min read

One screenshot from my GitHub contribution graph shows “1767 contributions on October 11th.”

I want that activity to create room for ideas I would otherwise put aside. It gives me a starting point for examining how I work, and a question to share: what does all that activity deliver?

Those are one day's recorded contributions, rather than features, customer outcomes or a daily average. The question I want the number to open is about the personal software factory behind it, and what a similar approach could make possible for your business.

I have two laptops and two PCs, multiple agents and subagents, and four or five projects running concurrently. Work continues around the clock. The tools are off the shelf; the method for connecting and directing them is the architecture I have put together.

Give the number a job it can do

Each project has a coordinating agent and subagents implementing work. I use GitHub issues for tracking, reviewed pull requests, automated tests and deployment pipelines. Forgejo supports my private work and deployments; GitHub supports public work and pipelines.

The graph makes that activity visible, subject to GitHub's contribution and visibility rules. The working records are where I look to understand what happened and what is ready for acceptance.

I still think the number is worth discussing. High activity creates a responsibility to understand the system producing it. If AI makes changes easier to generate, we need a clearer account of how those changes are selected and accepted.

The question for an organisation is therefore practical: can the people running delivery distinguish a busy day from a useful day using records they already have?

Start with accepted work

In my approach, I want a unit of work that can be accepted: a requirement satisfied, a defect resolved or a small increment ready for release. The issue needs to say what evidence would support that decision. You can apply that principle to a business service just as readily as a personal project.

For a request form, accepted work could mean that the intended user can submit the correct information, receive a usable result and recover from a failed attempt. The implementation, tests and review should support that claim. Creating a branch or opening a pull request represents a different stage.

I use VibeGov policies with ChatGPT to keep ownership, checks and completion claims explicit. They govern how the agents work within my factory. The repositories and pipelines carry the changes through implementation, review and release.

It also protects useful preparation from being mislabelled. Clarifying an uncertain requirement can be valuable progress even though it is not a released feature. Count it honestly in the appropriate place.

Put quality and delay beside the total

A daily delivery view should tell us what moved and what that movement cost the system.

How many increments were accepted? How many failed validation? How much previously accepted work was reopened? Which correction interrupted a planned change? Where is the oldest item waiting, and who can unblock it?

Include lead time, with an agreed start and finish. An organisation can report a fast implementation while the same change spends days awaiting a decision or release approval. Measuring only the active coding period hides that experience from the people using the service.

Look at work in progress as well. Ten simultaneous changes may mean ten opportunities to finish, or ten sets of assumptions awaiting integration. The queue, its age and its dependencies help distinguish those situations.

Give value a separate conversation

Accepted delivery still needs a connection to the reason for doing the work.

Did somebody stop entering the same information twice? Could a service team handle a request more reliably? Was an identified failure mode removed? Where an outcome cannot yet be measured, say what will be checked and when, rather than filling the gap with a convenient contribution total.

Some worthwhile changes improve maintainability or reduce a risk that has not materialised. They need their own explanation and evidence. Forcing every increment into an invented revenue figure makes the scorecard less trustworthy.

Ask the person accountable for the outcome to participate. A delivery team can prove a defined behaviour works. The organisation still has to decide whether that behaviour was worth providing.

Use daily signals without creating a daily contest

A daily view is useful for spotting a blocked queue or a failing integration path. It is a weak basis for ranking people whose assignments have different risks, dependencies and visibility.

Review patterns over time. Examine comparable types of work. Include rework and the effort required to sustain the pace. A burst of contribution activity does not establish that the same rate is desirable, repeatable or healthy.

If a target rewards visible activity alone, people and automated systems can learn to produce visible activity. Before adopting a measure, ask what behaviour it encourages and what it leaves out. Keep the underlying examples available so someone can challenge the interpretation.

What would you put beside 1,767?

My factory is where I am making the open-source framework repeatable on my tiny budget. I want observations from each project to inform reusable policies, patterns and tooling. Documenting the reasoning helps me learn what belongs in the shared foundation and what remains specific to one task.

An organisation should be able to point to accepted changes, the evidence behind them, the work still waiting and the outcomes it expects to improve. That is a more demanding account than a bright contribution graph, and a more useful one.

What useful software would you want your factory to deliver? Try a bounded change with the shared approach and record its outputs and the outcome you sought. If you are already doing something similar, share your measures and the foundation you reuse. I wonder what others are learning from their setups. Comparing methods and findings gives us more to work with than a contribution total alone.

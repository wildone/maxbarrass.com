42,850 Contributions: The Year I Changed How I Build Software.

My GitHub screenshot says “42,850 contributions in 2026”.

By my own estimate, that is roughly twice the recorded contribution activity of my previous sixteen years combined. It marks a striking change in how I work.

That is 2026 so far, and recorded activity rather than a count of useful releases or business value. The exciting part is the personal software factory behind it.

I am building this factory so I can chase my dreams without leaving them on the backburner. The tools are off the shelf; my method is open source and evolving. I am documenting how I work so others can try it and share what they learn.

I have work running around the clock, across a setup of two laptops and two PCs using multiple agents and subagents. I have four or five projects running at once. Coordinating that work is a different problem from getting one useful answer out of one conversation. Each contributor needs to know what it owns, what it can change and how its result reaches the next person or agent.

The unit of attention has changed

When AI can help produce an implementation, the question becomes what work to put in front of it and what must come back.

I find the factory idea useful because it forces the surrounding work into view. Someone must understand the request, connect it to requirements, decide the boundaries and determine what evidence would justify accepting the result. Someone must also follow through when the work encounters a failing test, a missing permission or an unresolved product decision.

That shifts my attention towards the delivery system. A plausible answer is only one item moving through it. I want the intended behaviour, the implementation, the checks and the resulting repository state to remain connected.

I am making that framework repeatable, extracting reusable knowledge, patterns and tooling into a shared foundation. Lessons from one project should inform the next, with agents following consistent conventions and workflows instead of rebuilding the same arrangements.

Experience needs a usable form

Experience is useful when another contributor can act on it. An architectural decision buried in an old conversation offers little help to an agent making the next change.

VibeGov is the set of policies I use with ChatGPT to clarify work, establish ownership, preserve checkpoints and require evidence. It gives my accumulated engineering practice a form agents can follow. My factory is the wider arrangement of people, agents, repositories and pipelines.

That does not make judgement automatic. A written rule can be incomplete. A requirement can be wrong. A validator can miss an important failure. The value of making the work explicit is that somebody else has a better chance of inspecting and challenging it.

I want that challenge to be possible without requiring a new person to absorb the entire history of a project. A useful handoff should explain the current state, the decisions that matter, the unresolved risks and the next authorised step.

The factory uses tools you can get

Each project has a coordinating agent, with subagents handling implementation. I track work through GitHub issues and have pull requests reviewed. Automated tests and deployment pipelines form part of the route from a proposed change to something I can assess.

I use a local Forgejo server for private work and deployments, and GitHub for public work and pipelines. These are accessible building blocks. You can choose an arrangement suited to your own projects, access requirements and budget.

The work around Governance.Foundation keeps authority and accountability in view. The Service Lasso guides make service dependencies and recovery behaviour concrete. Those bodies of guidance help me ask better questions while the tools carry out the work.

The opportunity for your business is to connect its knowledge to a similar delivery loop. Pick something people understand well but keep postponing. Give agents bounded work, a route for questions and a way to demonstrate completion.

More activity raises the standard for measurement

The contribution total gives me activity to examine. I want to understand what is useful enough to repeat.

I want to observe the outputs behind it: accepted changes, verified requirements and work needing correction. Then I want to examine outcomes: did the software do something useful for the person using it? Those are questions for my records and experiments, rather than results the graph can establish.

A large amount of recorded activity can include valuable learning and necessary maintenance. It can also include repeated repairs. Without examining the work, the same bright graph can support several different stories.

My sixteen-year comparison remains a personal estimate of recorded activity, rather than a controlled productivity study. I want the work behind it open to useful questions.

An organisation considering AI needs the same discipline. Before claiming an improvement, decide which result should improve and which evidence will show it. Preserve the inconvenient numbers as well as the attractive ones.

The next chapter needs a better question

I do not want the next stage of this journey to become a competition to make the graph darker. That would be a very efficient way to optimise a picture.

I want a clearer connection between what I ask the system to do, what it changes and what another person can accept as complete. I want work to survive a handoff, an exhausted allowance or a new contributor without losing its purpose.

AI creates room to attempt more. The responsibility is to use some of that room for better requirements, stronger checks, useful documentation and a system that can recover when an attempt fails.

The graph records movement from my personal factory on my tiny budget. I am sharing how I work and what I am learning as I improve the reusable foundation. Try a bounded piece of work, compare outputs and examine its outcome. I wonder what others are doing with their resources. Share your method and observations so we can learn from the differences.

When a Good Story Outruns the Evidence

By Max Barrass · 12 November 2026 · 3 min read

There is a particular kind of pitch that makes me want to stop the conversation and ask: what are we actually being asked to believe?

The problem disappears. The technology sounds impressive. Someone with a title assures us it works. We are already discussing implementation before anyone has properly examined the evidence.

I want a better way to slow that moment down.

That is the starting point for my working paper, Convenience, Mystique and Unsupported Authority. It develops the Convenience–Mystique–Fiducial framework, or CMF, as a way to examine how the appeal of a claim can get ahead of its justification.

The framework asks three questions:

  • Convenience: What effort, discomfort or unwanted consequence does this claim promise to remove?
  • Mystique: What makes the explanation compelling, and is that appeal helping us understand the evidence or distracting us from its absence?
  • Unsupported authority: What are we being asked to accept on someone else’s assurance, and is there a credible way to check it?

None of these features makes a claim false. Useful products save effort. Good explanations are engaging. Experts often deserve our trust. The concern is what happens when those things take the place of scrutiny.

Imagine a vendor promising that its AI platform will halve your operating costs within three months. Half of which costs? Compared with what baseline? Does the calculation include integration, oversight and mistakes? Has the result been demonstrated in an organisation like yours?

CMF is a conceptual framework under development, not a proven bullshit detector. The important test is whether it helps people make better decisions than existing approaches or a simpler checklist. That remains to be established.

I want the framework held to the same standard it asks of other claims: define it clearly, examine the evidence and leave room for it to be wrong.
